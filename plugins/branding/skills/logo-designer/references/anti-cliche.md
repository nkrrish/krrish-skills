# Anti-Cliché

The default output of any logo generator is the most-represented mark in its training data. That
is the definition of a cliché, and it is why generated logos for the same category look
identical. This file exists to make the obvious answer unavailable.

---

## Part 1: Banned first ideas, by domain

If the product is in one of these categories, the listed marks are **forbidden in every concept**.
Not "used sparingly" — forbidden. They are the first thing everyone draws, which is exactly why
they cannot be ownable.

| Domain | Banned |
|---|---|
| AI / ML | Sparkles, four-point stars, neural node graphs, brains, hexagon grids, glowing orbs |
| Audio / voice | Three-to-five bar equalizers, symmetric sine waveforms, microphones, headphones, sound-radiating arcs |
| Chat / messaging | Speech bubbles of any kind, paper planes, envelopes |
| Security | Shields, padlocks, keys, fingerprints, eyes |
| Analytics / data | Bar charts, upward arrows, pie segments, line graphs with a peak |
| Cloud / infra | Cloud outlines, servers, stacked discs, globes with latitude lines |
| Productivity | Checkmarks, clipboards, calendars, lightbulbs, rockets, gears |
| Finance | Upward arrows, coins, dollar glyphs, bulls, columns |
| Health | Hearts, crosses, leaves, water drops |
| Dev tools | Angle brackets, terminal prompts, braces, forked-path git graphs |
| Social | Interlocking rings, people-cluster silhouettes, hand waves |

**The test:** if you can name three shipping products using that mark, it is not available to you.

## Part 2: Route around the cliché

When the obvious metaphor is banned, these are the productive moves — in rough order of how often
they produce something ownable:

1. **Depict the adjacent moment, not the thing.** Not the voice — the pause before it. Not the
   message — the gap where a reply hasn't arrived. Not encryption — the shape of a thing that
   cannot be entered. The adjacent moment is almost always less occupied than the thing itself.
2. **Depict the artifact, not the act.** A transcription tool is not a microphone; it is the seam
   where speech becomes text. Find the residue the product leaves behind.
3. **Depict the constraint.** What can this product not do? Marks built on a limit — a fixed
   window, a single channel, a hard edge — read as confident and specific.
4. **Steal from an unrelated discipline.** Cartography, weaving drafts, musical notation, circuit
   diagrams, knot theory, botanical keys, semaphore, tide tables. The vocabulary is unexploited
   in software branding precisely because nobody looks there.
5. **Invert the figure and ground.** Draw the hole rather than the object. This alone rescues most
   banned metaphors — a "speech bubble" as the void inside a solid mass is no longer a speech bubble.
6. **Take the metaphor literally past the point of comfort.** If the product types for you, the
   mark is a key being pressed by nothing. Push a literal reading until it becomes strange.

## Part 3: The conceptual seed

Every concept carries **one sentence** naming the specific idea it encodes — written before the
SVG, not reverse-engineered after it.

A good seed is a claim about *this* product that would be false of its competitors:

- Weak: "Represents voice and text." True of every transcription tool. Not a seed.
- Strong: "The mark is the shape of the silence the app is listening through."
- Strong: "The mark is a cursor that has been interrupted mid-blink."
- Strong: "The mark is one keystroke that was never physically pressed."

The seed is a **subtle reference, not a caption**. Someone who knows the product should feel it;
everyone else should simply see a strong mark. If the mark has to be explained to work, the seed
has become an illustration and the concept has failed.

State the seed when presenting each concept. The user is choosing between ideas.

## Part 4: Risk allocation — the 3+1+1 rule

Five safe concepts is a failure, even when all five are competent. A round with no risk in it
gives the user nothing to react against, and reactions are how briefs get sharpened.

Every round of five is allocated:

- **Three ON-BRIEF.** Different lineages, different constructions, all directly answering the
  brief. These are the ones likely to ship.
- **One BREAKS A STATED PREFERENCE.** Deliberately violate one *soft* preference — the stated
  style, the palette, the expected weight. Never violate a hard constraint (legibility at 16px,
  monochrome survival, no lettering when forbidden). Label it plainly: "This one ignores your
  preference for X, because Y."
- **One OPPOSITE TEMPERAMENT.** If the brief asks for restrained and technical, this one is warm
  and strange. If it asks for playful, this one is austere. Not a worse mark — an equally
  crafted mark arguing for a different personality.

Present all five without ranking them. The risky two are frequently what the user actually wants
once they can see it; more often they clarify the brief by being wrong in a specific, useful way.

**Do not quietly drop the risky two because they feel less safe to show.** That instinct is the
thing this file exists to defeat.

## Part 5: The reduction pass

Before any concept is shown, take one more pass. The instruction is **subtractive only**.

If your instinct is to add a shape, stop and ask instead: *what can come out and leave the idea
intact?* Then do that. A mark that has survived a deliberate attempt to remove parts of it is the
only kind that looks inevitable rather than assembled.

Specifically, on every concept:

- Remove any element that does not carry the seed
- Merge any two elements doing the same job
- Push the negative space wider until just before the form breaks, then step back one increment
- Check that every remaining curve is either a true arc or a deliberate departure from one

The finished mark should look like it was arrived at, not composed — the product of someone who
drew it forty times and kept the fourth. That impression is the whole difference between a mark
that ships and a mark that gets regenerated.
